# Origins: the roleplay

> Upstream of everything. Before *Jeremy & Robert* (Robert's RM2K game, ~2003), before
> [*J&R II: Disaster Of Time*](./prequel-jr2.md), before Chef Adventure — there were two kids in a dark
> bedroom making it up out loud. Recorded 2026-07-31 from Jeremy's account.

**This is not canon.** It is the source layer: where the characters, the cosmology, and — most importantly —
the *thesis* actually came from. Nothing here binds Chef Adventure. It explains it.

## What it was

Jeremy met Robert at **age 9** — a neighborhood friend, a couple of blocks down the street in **West
Seattle** — and they were close by **10**. Sleepovers were routine, and the thing they did on those nights,
which Robert's mother disapproved of, was **roleplay**.

Lights out. Robert on the bed, Jeremy on the floor. No dice, no books, no system. **Purely narrated**: each
boy spoke for his own character and took turns narrating the world at the other — what you see, who you meet,
what happens next. Effectively D&D with the mechanics removed and nothing left but fiction.

The two characters they played were **Jeremy** and **Robert** — themselves. That is the whole origin of the
protagonists: not designed, *inhabited*.

**It ran for years.** The Jeremy and Robert of the final sessions are continuously the same characters as the
first — never rebooted, never re-rolled. That continuity is the reason "the turn" below happened at all.

**The last session** was most likely in Jeremy's basement room in the **Burien** house — after the family had
moved out of West Seattle, in a room where the two of them could be alone and talk freely. There was no
final-session ceremony; it simply stopped happening. Jeremy was around 15.

Every one of these predates all three games and entered them from the bedroom, not from a design document.

### The morph system

**A J&R1 mechanic. *Disaster Of Time* had no morphs at all.**

Jeremy could transform, and **the morphs carried the elemental abilities** — that was their mechanical
purpose, not just spectacle.

**Important qualifier** `LOCKED (2026-07-31)`: there was **no one-morph-per-element scheme**, and the source
creatures were **not** "elementals" in the Chef Adventure sense. They were simply monsters Jeremy encountered
in the fiction and decided to become — *"I'm gonna be that."* Do not read CA's six-element structure back
into them, and do not treat the unclaimed **energy** element as having a prequel occupant. How energy gets
covered in CA is undecided and unrelated.

The model was **Final Fantasy VII's Vincent Valentine**, whose limit break turns him into demons, with one
deliberate difference:

> **Vincent's transformed state plays itself. Jeremy's was player-controlled.**

Two are remembered:

- **Raving Lunatic** — canon in CA. A post-game morph atop a mountain: Jerald with the filters off and the
  stats way up, later permanently fused, which canon says clouds his cognition.
- **Jragyn** — a dragon form. The spelling is nine-year-old Jeremy needing a cooler way to write "dragon."

Any others are `LOST`.

**Provenance note.** [story-canon.md](./story-canon.md) cites `jragyn = dragon` as an example of **void clown
naming grammar**, alongside `Majik = magic`. In origin it was neither: Robert said *"and you have a dragon
morph,"* and Jeremy replied that it would be spelled **like this** — Robert then put that spelling in the
game. It is also Jeremy's real-life handle to this day.

**The example stays.** `LOCKED (2026-07-31)` Jeremy's position: this is unremarkable as fantasy naming goes —
Final Fantasy XIV romanizes half its proper nouns into things that read like a translation tool's first
guess. A nine-year-old's cooler spelling of "dragon" is squarely within genre convention, and it does not
need to be explained or excused in canon.

### Kore

Robert's character. A cat-humanoid who fought with claws. He was part of the story, and then **abruptly
disappeared from it mid-adventure.** Jeremy assumes he died; the actual events are `LOST`.

**Jeremy's explicit decision (2026-07-31): this stays as-is.** Kore's death is not to be invented, filled in,
or dramatized just because the record is blank. CA already honors him — Rupert's *Methodical Dismantler* fork
is *"a tribute to Kore — a cat-humanoid who fought with claws. Rupert learned to fight like him,"* and the
**Avenge** signature carries *"For Kore."* That memorial stands on an absence, deliberately.

### Melissa and Angela

A matched pair — Jeremy's and Robert's respective love interests from the same sessions. Melissa was
lovey-dovey but **deadly serious about the sanctity of her forest**. Angela was written as genuinely strong,
"a tier to her own," beneath the two protagonists but above everything else. Angela may have been the fourth
recruit J&R2 never built, though that is unconfirmed.

**Neither belongs in Chef Adventure.** `LOCKED (2026-07-31)` Melissa is not in CA and will not be, and
Angela follows her out by extension — **because CA's romantic character is based on Jeremy's fiancée.** The
childhood love interests are not being retrofitted over someone real.

## The turn

The single most important thing in this document — and it was **not a moment. It was a drift.**

The roleplay began the way every JRPG begins: two ordinary hero-shaped protagonists doing good and climbing.
Over years of continuous play they **ascended the echelons of godhood**. And as Jeremy puts it: *the stronger
they got, the more they tried to push the boundaries and get away with nonsense.*

There was no session where they decided to become monsters. Escalation did it. A representative exchange:

> **Narrator:** "The thief steals your gold pouch and starts running into the crowd. What do you do?"
>
> **Jeremy:** "I throw my battleaxe at him from 100 meters away and cleave him in half, then walk up casually
> and grab my pouch."
>
> **Narrator:** "...okay... you throw your battle axe, and because of your raw strength, it spins with enough
> force to generate its own gravity and sucks in a lot of random pedestrians — all of which ALSO get cleaved
> into bits. You basically just threw a lethal blender, and while you did succeed in killing the thief, you
> also decimated the village population."
>
> **Jeremy:** "Cool, I search all their blender'd bits and pieces and grab their loot, too."

Read what actually happens there. The narrator **escalates the consequence to punish the overreach** — the
village is dead, this was supposed to land as horror. And the player simply... **absorbs it and loots the
bodies.** The moral weight is offered and declined, on the spot, without hesitation.

The retrospective version of that question — *"wait, why are we still doing the good stuff? We can do
literally whatever we want and no one can stop us"* — is the summary of a conclusion arrived at through
hundreds of small exchanges exactly like this one.

**That is the birth of Jerald and Rupert as Chef Adventure knows them.** Not a design choice made later for
tonal contrast — a place two kids ended up by playing the power fantasy honestly, all the way to its end, and
noticing what was on the other side.

Everything downstream follows:

- The void clown genocide happens *after* the turn. Two beings who had already decided morality was optional
  wiped out a species and moved on.
- J&R2 opens with them mugging a guard for fun and murdering two workmen over an interrupted nap, as comedy.
- CA's thesis — *"everyone is the protagonist of their own story,"* the game never adjudicating, Grudj's
  righteous vengeance being *Tuesday* from the other side — is the adult articulation of the village the
  battleaxe went through.

The protagonists were never written as villains. They were **played into** villainy by two children who
followed the genre's own logic further than the genre does.

## Why this matters for Chef Adventure

- **The characterization is not a bit.** It is twenty-five years old, arrived at organically, and has never
  wavered. When Jerald does something monstrous and funny, that is the character, not a joke about the
  character.
- **Grudj's grievance is legitimate,** and the game's refusal to adjudicate it comes from authors who made the
  protagonists indefensible by accident, decades ago, and then kept loving them anyway.
- **The narrator's role is the player's role.** In the roleplay, one boy kept raising the cost and the other
  kept declining to pay it. CA puts the player in the second chair.
- **Kore is the only roleplay creation already load-bearing in CA**, and he got there as a memorial rather
  than a character.
